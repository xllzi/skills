# Study Planing

Turn a learning intention into a justified, actionable, revisable route. Prefer the structure of established courses, books, and projects; adapt that structure to the learner's goal rather than generating a curriculum from scratch.

The learner chooses the direction and produces understanding. Planning organizes resources, dependencies, and commitments. Learning evidence determines whether the route still fits.

## Choose the entry point

- **Assess:** judge suitability and explain the relevant tradeoffs. Stop with a recommendation or bounded trial when that satisfies the request; do not automatically produce a full plan.
- **Plan:** organize selected material into stages, pacing, and an initial learning entry.
- **Revise:** identify what changed and revise the affected decisions without restarting assessment or rebuilding the whole route.

Locate the study project from the goal, materials, and prior context; it may differ from the working directory. Read any existing `.study-guide/PLAN.md`, the relevant route and resume point in `PROGRESS.md`, and decision-relevant evidence in `STATE.md`. Reuse established goals, choices, and progress. Ask only for missing information that would change a planning decision; unknown proficiency is not a demonstrated prerequisite gap.

## Assess fit

Establish the desired capability, why it matters, what is outside scope, and constraints that affect the route. Distinguish a primary learning goal from additional goals that add effort, such as learning a new programming language alongside an algorithm. Do not require precise weekly hours when the learner has no such constraint.

Inspect the actual source: outcomes, prerequisites, sequence, representative material and exercises, projects, and available feedback or verification. Record the course edition or offering when it changes those facts. Distinguish source-supported facts, learner reports, and planning estimates; state inaccessible or unverified material explicitly.

Judge goal coverage, prerequisite distance, feasible effort, and opportunities to practice and verify. Recommend direct study, selected parts, bounded preparation, a trial, or a different resource with reasons. Avoid a general entrance quiz or collecting alternatives after there is enough evidence to choose.

When fit depends on actual learning, propose a representative trial with a bounded source location and the decision its result will inform. Hand the learning itself to study-guide when available. Do not declare fit, mastery, or trial completion before evidence exists, or make a full long-term plan a prerequisite to the trial.

## Build the route

Choose a main resource or coherent main sequence. Give each supplement a specific role and entry condition: prerequisite support, practice/project, or reference. Keep only choices and consequential rejected alternatives that explain the route; do not turn search results into a reading backlog.

Preserve useful source sequencing and prerequisite relationships. For scattered sources, explain how each stage prepares for the next. A stage represents a meaningful capability or project outcome, not necessarily a chapter or week.

For each stage, specify its outcome, coarse material scope, dependencies, and observable completion criterion. Detail the current stage enough to begin; keep later stages coarse until the next need becomes concrete. Separate estimates from hard deadlines, check whether the requested scope fits the available time, and use trial experience to calibrate effort. Without a date requirement, use stage order and a sustainable effort range rather than an invented daily calendar.

When creating or updating a persistent plan, read [the plan schema](references/plan-schema.md). A plan should expose the current stage, an initial source entry, completion criteria, and review conditions. It should not duplicate the exact material checklist or the learner's current action.

## Revise from evidence

Compare the changed circumstances with actual progress and capability evidence. Distinguish reduced available time, prerequisite difficulty, deliberate deeper study, resource problems, and changed goals before selecting an adjustment. Revise only the affected scope, resources, stage order, or pacing; preserve completed work and its evidence.

Reassess at a meaningful stage boundary, repeated prerequisite obstruction, sustained effort mismatch, unavailable core material, or a changed goal. One difficult attempt usually needs local teaching support. A learner's request to skip or move on is not automatically a gap or a review obligation.

Apply changes the learner has already requested. If proposing a different goal, main resource, substantial scope cut, or substantial new stage beyond that authorization, show the concrete choice and consequences before adopting it. Routine local adaptation and estimate updates do not need repeated confirmation. Keep an unchosen alternative separate from the active route.

Record the latest substantive revision and why it matters; do not build a turn-by-turn history. If the route still fits, say so without rewriting it merely to show activity.

## Finish and hand off

For assessment, return the recommendation, decisive evidence, uncertainty, and any useful trial. For planning, return the route and first entry. For revision, return the change, its evidence, and its consequences. Keep operational bookkeeping in the artifacts.

Read [the study-guide handoff](references/study-guide-handoff.md) when connecting a plan to learning or consuming a planning review. A plan-only request ends when the route is usable. If the learner already asked to start studying, continue with study-guide without another generic confirmation. No separate chat or agent is required. When study-guide is unavailable, leave a usable source entry and criteria without fabricating learning records.
